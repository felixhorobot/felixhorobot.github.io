01

Visual inspection with numbers a customer signs off on

Pack outfeed before wrapping: every stacked pack must be counted, its printed markings read, and its timber grade confirmed. It was done by eye. I replaced it with deep learning OCR and tally counting across 3 cameras, including a Cognex In-Sight for the first-layer OCR read.

In production it holds 100% OCR read accuracy and 99% tally counting accuracy over an average 350 stacked packs per shift. The machine learning grade confirmation replaced the manual visual check, cut average station time by 30 seconds, and reduced customer refund requests.

Those numbers are not from a test set. They are measured on the line, across shifts, with the mismatches logged and reviewed. That is the standard I hold vision systems to: the number that matters is the one production sees.

02

Real-world imperfect data is my normal data

Timber is a natural resource. No two boards match: grain, knots, colour from pale to nearly black, and surface reflectance that changes completely between wet and dry stock. Lines run at various speeds, up to 120 boards per minute. Your ad asks for someone comfortable with imperfect data; this is about three years of it.

My edge-case method is systematic, not ad hoc:

  • Mismatch records. Whenever a model output does not match the expected value, I keep the record and use it for review and retraining, especially edge conditions.
  • Tracing misses to the dataset. I traced residual counting misses to edge conditions not represented in the training data, then specified the exact data collection needed to close the gap.
  • A purpose-built annotation app. Frame extraction from production video, box, polygon and OCR labelling, one-click training export. Labelling fits the factory's data, not the other way around.
  • Material-driven sensing choices. When a feedback sensor was needed, testing showed laser misreads darker texture and wet timber, so I chose ultrasonic and allowed for its slower response in the control logic. The material decides, not the datasheet.
  • Optics from first principles. Field of view derived from working distance and target size, validated against manufacturer specs, checkerboard calibration, lighting and positioning tuned per install.
Your ad's line "lighting, camera positioning, changing products and manufacturing environments" is a list of problems I have already solved at least once each.

03

Speed: profiled and measured, never guessed

The platform averages 20-35 ms per inference on edge industrial PCs, soft real-time on a general-purpose OS, stated honestly. I built a stage-by-stage timing pipeline that benchmarks everything from image and video preprocessing through inference, so the bottleneck is found by measurement.

The investigation that shows how I work: two trigger-mode cameras that normally answered in 40-80 ms began sitting at about 135 ms. The obvious suspect, a newly added feature, was cleared by evidence: it was disabled, and its code was newer than the running process. The logs pointed at a removed always-on model that had quietly kept the GPU clocks pinned. Idle, the memory clock fell from 13,801 MHz to 405 MHz, a 34x drop, and YOLO inference is memory-bandwidth bound.

The fix, a keep-alive inference through the real model, was tuned entirely by measurement:

keep-alive rateresult
1 / secondunstable
4 / second18.9 ms
10 / secondno further gain
131.6 ms down to 22.1 ms and 137.1 down to 19.6, for about 10 W. Faster than the system had ever run, because the keep-alive warms the model that does the real work.

04

The full CV/ML lifecycle, owned by one engineer

Your ad describes the whole lifecycle. I run every stage of it myself, in production:

  • Data: collection from production video, the custom annotation app, mismatch-driven dataset growth.
  • Models: YOLO v8-v11 and Detectron2 in PyTorch: object detection, instance segmentation, multi-label classification, OCR. ONNX where it fits. TensorFlow/Keras and scikit-learn where they fit better.
  • Anomaly detection: skewed-stock and tangle detection on the log infeed catches faults before a blockage; that line is automated so operators only monitor it.
  • Deployment: Docker containers on edge industrial PCs I specified and built; automated startup, redundancy backup and self-recovery; covering two shifts, 20 hours a day, 5 days a week. A separate 9-camera deployment on the Daemon AI platform, where I selected the Axis cameras, configured IDIS recording, trained and deployed the models.
  • Actuation: I also write the Rockwell PLC programs, so inference output stops a line, raises an alarm or adjusts equipment over EtherNet/IP. The model's answer reaches the machine.
  • Visibility: a Flask app streams every feed with detection overlays and serves a REST API; results land in SQL and Power BI through Ignition SCADA.

05

Evaluation discipline: how models earn the right to run

Nothing I build goes live on a feeling. Acceptance criteria are set by the goal, accuracy or efficiency, before work starts. Pilots run on small datasets first. A test set is kept aside with mAP, precision and recall targets. Then a live trial on the line before sign-off, and an operator feedback loop into retraining after it. Experiments are tracked in MLflow, with Databricks across my model work, including the deployed YOLO models and my entry in the RSNA Knee Abnormality Detection challenge. Monitoring stays on after go-live; the mismatch records from section 02 are the continuous-iteration engine your ad asks about.
